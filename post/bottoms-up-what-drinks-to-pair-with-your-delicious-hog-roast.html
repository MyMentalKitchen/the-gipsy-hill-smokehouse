Bottoms up: what drinks to pair with a hog roast

Pork fat wants something to cut it: acid, bubbles, bitterness or cold. How to choose wine, beer, cider and alcohol-free drinks for a hog roast, and why the answer in a field in July isn't the answer at a dinner table.

Most wine advice for a hog roast says the same thing: a full-bodied red with plenty of tannin. At a dinner table in November, fair enough. At three o'clock in a marquee in July, a big red at room temperature is warm soup by the second glass, and the bottles go back to the kitchen half full.

The pork doesn't need weight. It has plenty of its own. What it needs is something to cut through the fat, the same job the apple sauce does on the plate. Acid does that. Bubbles do it. Hop bitterness does it. So does cold. Choose the drinks to pair with a hog roast for that job first, then for the weather, and most of the decisions make themselves.

What goes with hog roast: the drink does the apple sauce's job

Roast pork is rich and slightly sweet. The crackling is salt and fat. The drink that works is the one that resets your mouth between bites, so the fifth mouthful of pork tastes as good as the first.

That rules out less than you'd think. Plenty of wine, beer and cider can do it. What it rules out is anything heavy, anything sweet and anything warm, which is where most hog roast drinks go wrong.

What wine goes with hog roast

Red wine with hog roast

Tannin grips fat, which is where the full-bodied advice comes from, and it isn't wrong. For an autumn or winter party, with the pork carved indoors, a Chianti, a Rioja or a Malbec will stand up to it. The herbs on the pork matter too. Rosemary, fennel and garlic pull towards Italian and Spanish reds.

For an outdoor event in summer, go lighter. Pinot Noir, Beaujolais or a soft Merlot have enough acid to cut the pork without the weight. Serve them cool, around 12 to 14°C: half an hour in the fridge, or ten minutes in the ice bucket. A red that goes on the table slightly too cold warms up in the glass. One that starts warm only gets worse.

White wine with roast pork

White is the easier match, and the one people don't expect. When we looked at The Wine Society's food and wine matcher for hog roast in September 2026, two of the three grapes it put forward were white: Riesling and Chenin Blanc, with Merlot the only red.

That makes sense once you think about the fat. A dry or off-dry Riesling has sharp acid and a little fruit that sits happily next to apple sauce. Chenin Blanc does the same job with a bit more body. Look for wines with apple and citrus in them. Apple and pork have shared a plate for a long time, and the glass can carry the same idea.

Portuguese wines with roast pork

Pork sits at the centre of Portuguese cooking, and the wines grew up beside it. In Bairrada, the region famous for roast suckling pig (leitão), the pig is traditionally served with the local sparkling wine: cold, dry bubbles against crisp skin. It is the whole argument of this article in one glass.

Vinho Verde is light, sharp and often lower in alcohol than most whites, which makes it a good afternoon wine for a long party. For a red, the Alentejo in the south east makes ripe, soft reds that don't need a cellar to open up. Herdade do Esporão is one of the better-known estates and its wines are easy to find in the UK.

Sparkling wine and hog roast

Bubbles and acid both cut fat, which makes sparkling wine better with a hog roast than its reputation as a toasting drink suggests. It is also the natural reception drink, poured before anyone eats. Cava, crémant, Prosecco and English sparkling wine all work. Keep anything sweet for pudding.

Beer with hog roast

Beer may be the most natural drink with a hog roast. It is what people drink standing up, in a field, with a pork bap in the other hand. The fizz lifts the fat off your tongue and the hops cut through it.

A pale ale or a clean lager is the safe middle. A wheat beer, a German Weissbier such as Erdinger, is soft and slightly fruity and doesn't fight the pork. An IPA works when the pork has char on it and a punchy sauce beside it; the bitterness does the cutting. Darker beers, a brown ale or a porter, go with the smoky, blackened edges rather than the pale meat from the middle of the hog.

Buy local if you can. A local brewery's pale ale in cans, sitting in a tub of ice, is often the best drink at the whole party. Ours is Gipsy Hill Brewery, a few minutes down the road.

Cider with hog roast

Apple sauce on the plate, an apple in the pig's mouth, cider in the glass. It works for the same reason each time: sharp apple against sweet fat. (For the story behind the apple, see why an apple is placed in the mouth of a hog roast.)

Go dry or medium-dry. A sweet cider on a hot day next to rich pork is too much of both. A proper West Country or Herefordshire cider, or a Normandy cidre, sits between beer and wine and looks after the guests who don't want either.

Alcohol-free drinks with hog roast

The guests who aren't drinking deserve more than a jug of orange squash. The same rule applies to them: sharp, cold and not too sweet.

Iced tea works better than you'd guess, because tea has tannin, the same thing that lets red wine grip fat. Brew it strong, chill it, sweeten it lightly and put lemon in it. Sparkling apple juice cut with soda water does the cider's job. Alcohol-free beer is now good enough to go in the same tub as the rest. And water, on every table, all day. It is the drink people forget to plan and the one everybody wants by four o'clock.

Drinks for a hog roast party or wedding: plan them around the fire

This is the part a wine guide can't tell you, because at an event the drinks run to the fire, not the clock.

A whole hog is on for hours before anybody eats. Guests arrive, drink and wait. The reception is where the drinking starts, often in the sun, often on an empty stomach, and it runs longer than planned because the speeches always do. So the reception drink wants to be light and cold, and food needs to go round with it.

At a wedding we cooked in May for 109 guests (beef and chickens over the fire that day, not a hog), there were two receptions. First a quick wine tasting in the field straight after the ceremony. Then sparkling wine by the marquee, with the waiters taking empanadas round on trays. Nobody reached the meal on an empty glass or an empty stomach.

By the time the meat is carved, the table wine should already be down and cold. At that wedding it went on the tables in ice buckets, with water beside it, before the sharing platters came out. That is the table in the picture above. A bottle that turns up after the pork has missed the point of it.

Then the evening. Once the plates are cleared the drinking changes: less wine, more beer, cider and something to dance on. At that wedding the bar opened at eight with self-serve cocktails from three big containers, poured into enamel mugs with each guest's name on. Nothing to break on the grass, and nobody losing their drink. At nine, the job on our running order was topping up the cocktails, the ice and the water. Order more ice than you think you need.

Late-night pork baps, when they come out, go with a cold beer or a cider better than with anything else on the bar.

The one thing that catches everybody

Temperature. Not the grape, not the brewery. A cold lager beats an expensive warm red beside a hog roast in July. Keep the spare bottles in the shade, keep the ice coming, put water on every table and make sure there's something sharp in every glass.
